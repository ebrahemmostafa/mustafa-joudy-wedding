# Velvet Proscenium

A design philosophy for objects that are opened rather than read.

**The threshold is the work.** Everything in this language is organised around a moment of crossing: a flap lifting, a garden arch coming into focus, a strip of film sliding out of a slot, a gold skin scratched away. Composition is built around the gap at the centre of a symmetrical frame. The frame is heavy and sumptuous, the opening is quiet and bright, and the eye always lands in the light between the folds. Space is ceremonial and frontal, never casual: axes are dead centre, margins are generous, and the empty ivory field in the middle is treated as the most valuable surface on the page. A design here should look like it was composed slowly and with care, with every interval measured again until it felt inevitable.

**Three materials, never more.** Deep emerald velvet absorbs light and gives weight. Ivory stock carries the message, warm and slightly toothed, close to paper rather than screen-white. Antique gold, brushed, engraved and embossed, marks every point of contact: the rim of a medallion, the plate of a machine, the foil over a secret. Colour comes only from these materials, plus the painted garden that frames the names. Green is never a flat fill but a woven surface or a living leaf. Gold is never yellow paint but always lit metal. Every material must look rendered by someone who has handled the real thing: the nap of the velvet, the grain of the paper, the bevel of an engraving and the bloom of a watercolour petal, with a master's patience for surface.

**Typography as a hand and a voice.** Two registers only. A flowing script, used rarely and large, carries the names, like ink pulled by a steady pen. A refined old-style serif, light and often italic, speaks everything else in short, unhurried lines. Small capitals appear only where an engraver would put them: dates, times, the lettering on a plate. Words are few, set centred and balanced, and placed so that each one sits in its own pool of space. Text is never a block. Each line should feel set by hand, with kerning and leading adjusted by eye, as if fussed over by a typographer at the top of the craft.

**Scale through intimacy.** The largest element is always the most personal (the names), and the most precious objects are rendered small: a gold seal no larger than a coin, a monogram the size of a thumbprint, a date that appears only when earned. Rhythm comes from repetition of the frame motif at different scales: an arch inside a page, an oval inside a flap, a slot inside a plate, a circle inside a card. The same ceremony recurs at each size so the whole suite feels like one meticulously crafted object made by a single workshop.

**Restraint as luxury.** Ornament exists only where a real object would carry it. There are no decorative flourishes for their own sake and no gradients standing in for materials. One orchestrated movement per moment, then stillness. The finished piece should read as the product of deep expertise and countless hours of refinement: nothing crowded, nothing touching the edge, every element given room, the whole thing hushed and deliberate, like the second before a door opens onto a garden.
